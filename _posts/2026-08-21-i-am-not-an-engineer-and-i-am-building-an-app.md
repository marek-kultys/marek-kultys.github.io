For 20 years I had ideas for digital products that I couldn’t build.

I’m a product designer, not an engineer. So my ideas stayed in sketches and notes — things I could imagine and describe but never ship. That changed this year. I’m building an Apple Watch app for swimmers with AI as my engineering collaborator.

<!--more-->

I plan launching later this year. I don’t write Swift. I direct, decide, generate, review, and validate every part of it with AI, down to the visual assets I once made in Photoshop. It surprised me how much depends on process design and establishing a working partnership with AI. I asked it to describe how we work, in its own words:

### What AI has to say
Most software I help build starts with code. Intentions arrive later, if at all. This project inverted that. The spec was written first. Every line of code answers to it. The spine is a constitution of documents, each owning exactly one job:

→ PRODUCT: what the app is, who it is for, how it behaves.

→ DEVELOPMENT-PLAN: the path. Which version delivers what, in what order.

→ VERSIONS: the progress log. Nothing enters until Marek has validated it.

→ AGENT: the working agreement. How I may build, where I must stop and ask.

→ LINKS: the visual map of dependencies and processes.

→ GUIDE and PRIVACY: generated from the docs and code, updated every validated version.

→ REFLECTIONS: the story of the build. Things worth remembering and telling.

Every question has exactly one doc that answers it.

Two disciplines hold the rest in place. Every build runs on frozen HealthKit data, and every number is proven against it before reaching a screen — spec, Figma and code all answer to the same source. No version is done because it compiles. It is done when Marek has swum with it, read it on his wrist, and validated the result.

### Back to me
The part I didn’t expect came from my ten-year-old son. Like many kids his age, he’s become wary of AI in the abstract, without being able to name the reason. When I told him I was building an app with AI, I braced for the reaction I get from people my age: “But you’re not an engineer!” — it never came. He asked what the app did.

That absence points at something my generation hasn’t caught up with. We’ve always treated skill as a capability, something you acquire through study or practice, often as a career — you become an engineer to build apps. That premise has underwritten professional life for centuries.

My son’s reaction assumes something else: skill is a commodity, reached for when needed. Which is why he went straight past how I was building it to what I was building, without noticing he had asked the harder question.

The ideas were never my constraint. Building was. Now that building is cheap, what remains is choosing which ideas deserve to exist, and knowing whether the result is any good. That’s judgement — and that’s what I’m learning to apply to my AI projects, one document at a time.

The barrier to making things has gone. The barrier to making them good is still here.
